# Does legal professional privilege in EU competition law cover advice from a company's own in-house lawyers? How have the courts justified where the line falls, and what does that mean in practice during an inspection?

## Conclusion

Legal professional privilege in EU competition law does not cover advice from a company's own in-house lawyers, because the courts tie protection to the lawyer's independence from the client, so during a Commission inspection only external-lawyer communications can be withheld and disputes over a document's status are resolved through the sealed-envelope procedure pending court review.

Legal professional privilege under EU competition law does not extend to legal advice given by a company's own in-house lawyers.

The courts place the boundary of privilege at the lawyer's independence, treating any employment relationship as incompatible with that independence regardless of Bar membership, which is why external lawyers are covered and in-house lawyers are not.

In practice, during a Commission inspection a company can shield material discussed with external counsel but not advice from its in-house lawyers, and any dispute over a document's privileged status is resolved by sealing it pending challenge before the courts.

## Privilege coverage of in-house counsel

**AM & S independence test** — the conditions, in order (cumulative)[1][2] [commentary]:
1. the communication is made for the purpose and in the interests of the client's rights of defence
2. the lawyer is independent, not bound to the client by a relationship of employment, and entitled to practise in an EU or EEA Member State

The Court ruled that the requirement of independence implies the absence of any employment relationship between the lawyer and the client, so protection under the confidentiality principle does not extend to exchanges within a company or group with in-house lawyers.[3] [commentary] Because in-house lawyers are bound to their employer by an employment relationship, they cannot satisfy the independence condition of the test, so their communications with the company fall outside privilege. A Competition Policy Brief issued by members of the Commission's Competition Directorate in November 2025 reaffirms that legal professional privilege should not be extended to in-house lawyers.[4][5] [commentary]

## Judicial rationale for the boundary

Even where Member States' Bar ethical rules apply to in-house lawyers, those rules cannot guarantee the same level of independence as external lawyers, given the in-house lawyer's economic dependence and close ties with the employer.[6] [commentary] Court judgments require that the lawyer be both independent of the inspected company and subject to ethical rules as a member of a Bar or Law Society.[7] [commentary] Because employment inherently undermines independence and Bar membership alone cannot substitute for it, the courts have drawn the line so that only lawyers who are both independent of the client and subject to Bar discipline are covered, excluding in-house lawyers.

## Practical consequences during an inspection

The inspection is to be carried out in strict observance of legal professional privilege in relation to legal assistance provided before the inspection.[7] [commentary] The undertaking may consult external legal counsel during the inspection, but such counsel's presence is not a condition for the inspection's legality, and inspectors will accept only a short delay before proceeding.[7] [commentary] In Case T-125/03 & T-253/03, a mere cursory look by Commission officials at a document's general layout, heading, title or other superficial features will often let them confirm whether the document is confidential.[8] Where the Commission considers the material presented insufficient to prove confidentiality, in particular where the undertaking refuses a cursory look, officials may place a copy of the document in a sealed envelope and remove it for later resolution of the dispute.[8]

The company must request interim relief within the two-month deadline for challenging the Commission's order to produce a disputed document, since the courts have held that the Commission is prohibited from compelling the company to reveal the document's contents until that deadline expires, and the document may meanwhile be kept in a sealed envelope.[9] [commentary] In the most sensitive cases, in particular where the company argues a risk of serious violation of its rights such as privileged documents directly relating to the alleged infringement, the sealed-envelope procedure should be requested.[7] [commentary] Potentially privileged documents are examined separately from the main document review, between the company's external lawyers and the LPP experts.[7] [commentary] In Case C-550/07 P, a breach of legal professional privilege during an inspection occurs when a privileged document is seized by a Commission official, not when the Commission later relies on it in a decision on the merits.[10]
