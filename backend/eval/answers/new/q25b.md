# Does legal professional privilege in EU competition law cover advice from a company's own in-house lawyers? How have the courts justified where the line falls, and what does that mean in practice during an inspection?

## Conclusion

Legal professional privilege in EU competition law does not cover advice from a company's own in-house lawyers, protecting only communications with an independent lawyer not bound to the client by an employment relationship.[1]

Privilege in EU competition law protects only communications between a company and an independent lawyer, not advice from its own in-house lawyers.

Courts have drawn the boundary at the lawyer's independence from the client, excluding in-house lawyers because they are economically dependent on their employer.

In practice, once a document's privileged status is disputed during an inspection, the Commission must set it aside using the sealed-envelope procedure and cannot examine or rely on it until the dispute is formally resolved and any judicial review opportunity has passed.

## Privilege coverage of in-house counsel

Because in-house lawyers are bound to their employer by a relationship of employment, their advice does not satisfy the independence requirement and falls outside privilege.

**Conditions for legal professional privilege since AM&S Europe v Commission** — the conditions, in order (cumulative)[2] [commentary]:
1. the communications are made for the purpose and in the interests of the client's rights of defence
2. the lawyers are independent lawyers, not bound to the client by a relationship of employment, and entitled to practise their profession in an EU (or EEA) Member State

According to this commentary on the Commission's November 2025 policy brief, the Commission's practice of refusing to extend legal professional privilege to in-house lawyers is expected to remain unchanged even if a new procedural regulation is adopted.[3] [commentary]

## Judicial rationale for the boundary

In Case 155/79 (AM&S Europe v Commission), the Court of Justice held that confidentiality protection applies only where communications are exchanged between an independent lawyer, not bound to the client by a relationship of employment, and the client.[1] According to this commentary, the courts reasoned that in-house lawyers, being economically dependent on their employers, are not sufficiently independent to deal effectively with conflicts between their professional obligations, including collaboration with courts in the administration of justice, and their employer's aims and wishes.[4] [commentary] The courts therefore justified the privilege boundary by reference to the lawyer's structural independence from the client rather than professional qualification alone.

## Practical consequences during an inspection

In Case T-125/03 & T-253/03 (Akzo Nobel Chemicals and Akcros Chemicals v Commission), where a company does not satisfy the Commission that a document is confidential, in particular where it refuses to give Commission officials a cursory look at the document, the officials may place a copy in a sealed envelope and remove it pending resolution of the dispute.[5] If the Commission seizes a document claimed to be privileged without sealing it and without adopting a formal rejection decision, that act constitutes a tacit decision rejecting the privilege claim and allows immediate examination of the document.[5] Once the Commission adopts a decision rejecting a privilege claim, it must not read the document's content until the company has had the opportunity to refer the matter to the Court.[5] During the investigation at the applicants' premises, Commission officials, unable to reach a final conclusion on whether the documents should be privileged, took copies of them and placed them in a sealed envelope which they took away with them.[5] On appeal in Case C-550/07 P Akzo Nobel Chemicals and Akcros Chemicals v Commission, the Court held that a breach of legal professional privilege occurs at the moment the Commission's officials seize the document, not when the Commission later relies on it, so the applicants' interest in challenging the seizure continues for as long as the Commission holds the documents or copies.[6] During an inspection, officials will accept only a short delay for the company to consult its legal counsel before beginning to examine, copy or seal the business's books and records.[7] [commentary] Where the Commission decides not to maintain its request to access a sealed document it returns the envelope unopened; otherwise it gives the company advance notice of its decision to open it so the company can challenge that decision before the General Court and seek interim measures.[7] [commentary] Potentially privileged documents identified during document review are flagged and set aside so they can be examined separately by LPP experts and the company's external lawyers rather than by the ordinary inspection team.[7] [commentary] Where the Commission seizes a document without sealing it and without adopting a formal rejection decision, the resulting tacit rejection permits immediate examination only because no sealed-envelope procedure was engaged; once a document has instead been sealed, or a formal rejection decision has been adopted, the Commission remains barred from reading it until the company has had the opportunity to seek judicial review.
